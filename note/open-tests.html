Thirteen Tests of the Open

We mechanised the standard 9:30–11:00 playbook against 10.7 GB of 5-minute data. Six rounds of review found nine errors and reversed the headline four times before the estimand was right. Where it lands: the entries carry a real directional edge of roughly 1–2bp, against realistic costs of 3–6bp.

1,425 sessions 2021–2026 200 names, point-in-time 5-min bars, 04:00–20:00 ET

Every claim below came from trading education material — ICT-derived session logic, break-and-retest, opening range, fair value gaps. We mechanised each one, ran it across the full sample, and charged costs. The point of this note is not the scoreboard. It's the definitions section, and the benchmark section that follows the results: every entry we tested carries a small positive directional edge, and not one of them beats a rule that looks only at the sign of the last thirty minutes. If we operationalised "confirmation" or "displacement" differently from how you'd read it on a chart, the results are a verdict on our code, not on your method.

How we defined things

These are the judgement calls. Attack these first.

break
A 5-minute close beyond the level — not a wick through it. First occurrence between 09:30 and 11:00.
advance
Price must then run 0.18% past the level before a retest counts. Scaled from the daily version by bar range (daily bars ≈ 2.18%, 5-min ≈ 0.20%).
retest
At least 3 bars (15 min) after the break. Without this, 73% of our "retests" were the bar immediately after the breakout.
confirmation
The tapping bar closes green and closes back above the level. This is our reading of "price respects the FVG" — the part we're least sure of.
stop
Three constructions tested across experiments — 2 × the entry bar's range, below the level itself (both in E6), and 2 × ATR(14) (E10d). Differences under 0.01R, which is what a martingale predicts in R units.
levels
PDH, PDL, PDC, pre-market 04:00–09:30 high/low. All fixed before 09:30.
displacement
For FVGs: middle candle range ≥ 1.5× ATR, closing in the top/bottom third of its own range.
costs
Run at 1–2bp round trip. That is the quoted spread, not what you pay on a breakout entry or a stop into momentum — 3–6bp all-in is the defensible base case and we have not re-run at it. Resting stop, evaluated intrabar: triggered when the bar’s low breaches it, filled at the worse of the stop price and that same bar’s open, so gaps through are charged. Not stop-on-close. When one bar spans both stop and target the stop is assumed first. We have now counted these: 35 of 639,329 resolved trades, 0.005%. Running the whole study the other way changes nothing to four decimals, which is why it changes nothing.

The one that matters

Comparing a structured entry to a random one requires choosing how to weight trades, and the weights turned out to matter more than the signal. Two natural choices are biased in opposite directions:

estimand                              excess     bias
trade-weighted vs random              +0.011     UP   over-weights the majority
                                                      side, which is the side
                                                      the day rewarded
side-balanced vs random               -0.012     DOWN gives the minority side
                                                      equal weight; on a trend
                                                      day those are counter-
                                                      trend signals that lose
                                                      (and 1/n is not knowable
                                                      until 11:00)

The clean design pairs each signal with a random bar in the same name, session, side, risk unit and time neighbourhood, drawn at or after the signal. But because the placebo inherits the side, that difference holds direction fixed — it tests timing, not whether the signal picks the right side. Direction needs the signal's absolute gross R, since a random-side entry has zero expected gross R.

DIRECTION — signal's absolute gross R, vs a zero-expectation benchmark

entry        stop-first   target-first        t      ~bp of price
pullback        +0.0213      +0.0214      +2.73          +1.9
ORB             +0.0198      +0.0198      +1.53          +1.8
VWAP            +0.0152      +0.0153      +2.15          +1.4
level           +0.0075      +0.0075      +0.68          +0.7

TIMING — signal minus a delayed same-side entry

pullback        +0.0098   t=+1.91        ORB    +0.0006   t=+0.08
level           +0.0076   t=+1.09        VWAP   -0.0019   t=-0.48

Direction is positive. The entries pick the right side slightly more often than chance. Four entries were tested, so the honest statement is significant after multiplicity correction for one of the four: at Bonferroni α = 0.05/4 the critical |t| is 2.50, which pullback (2.73) clears and VWAP (2.15) does not. Timing adds nothing: entering at the signal is indistinguishable from entering a few bars later, on all four.

And the same-bar tie-break explains nothing — 35 trades out of 639,329, as the definitions section now records.

So: a gross directional edge of roughly 1–2bp of price, against realistic all-in costs of 3–6bp. Revisions 4 through 6 claimed these entries were worse than random; that was the side-balanced statistic, which error 07 disowns. But the size of the edge is no longer the interesting question — the next section is.

The benchmark that kills it

A positive directional edge only means something if it is specific to the pattern. So we built the crudest possible competitor: same entry bar, same stop, same 3R target, same costs — but the side is set by the sign of the last k bars and nothing else. No level, no break, no retest, no gap. If the pattern is doing work, it must beat this.

same bar, same stop, same target; side from the pattern vs side from momentum

entry      pattern   mom k=3   mom k=6   mom k=12    pattern - mom6       t
pullback   +0.0210   +0.0095   +0.0183   +0.0214          +0.0057     +0.90
ORB        +0.0198   +0.0198   +0.0198   +0.0346          +0.0000       n/a
VWAP       +0.0148   +0.0127   +0.0215   +0.0183          -0.0029     -0.59
level      +0.0086   +0.0060   +0.0084   +0.0278          +0.0040     +0.33

No entry beats the momentum rule. The differences are +0.0057, 0.0000, −0.0029 and +0.0040 R, none of them within reach of significance. The 1–2bp we spent six revisions measuring is generic opening continuation — the tendency of the first ninety minutes to keep going — and the patterns are ways of noticing it, not sources of it.

ORB's zero is exact, and it is the clearest result in the note. Its t is undefined because the difference is identically zero on every trade: an opening-range break upward is a positive sign of the recent move, so the momentum rule selects the same side, every time. ORB is not a pattern that happens to correlate with momentum. It is a momentum rule with a level drawn on top of it.

This is what we would most like a second opinion on. If the answer is "of course, the level is a filter, not the signal — it tells you where to put the stop, not which way to go," then we have been testing the wrong object all along, and the question becomes whether the level improves the risk unit rather than the direction. We have not tested that.

One number we had to reconcile

Review caught two ORB figures in earlier revisions that disagreed in sign — −0.0700 and +0.0198 — and was right that nothing in the section could be trusted until that was settled. Both were correct for their own specification, which was the problem: they were never the same statistic.

from the diagnostic's number to the main run's, one change at a time

  long only, 1bp cost, every 10th session     -0.0683
  + drop the cost charge                      -0.0534   (+0.015)
  + include the downside break, not just up   +0.0441   (+0.098)  <- the gap
  + use every 2nd session, not every 10th     +0.0173   (-0.027)  within noise

The diagnostic ran long breaks only, net of 1bp, on a tenth of the sample. Almost the whole sign flip is the missing short side. The remainder is sampling: the 10% subsample's own clustered SE is about 0.048.

The more useful fact is what the clustered errors look like once both sides are in:

That the two numbers could differ by 0.09 and both be unremarkable is the real lesson, and it applies to a great deal of published backtest disagreement.

The layers we could not test

Review made the strongest argument in this whole exchange: the entry rule is the least important part of what a profitable trader does, and popularity is no evidence at all. The population studies are unambiguous — Taiwan 1992–2006, full trade records (Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean, Do Day Traders Rationally Learn About Their Ability?): about 20% profit after a reasonable accounting, and fewer than 3% of active day traders on an average day earn predictably positive net returns. Brazil (Chague, De-Losso & Giovannetti): of individuals who day-traded futures on more than 300 days, 97% lost money; 1.1% earned more than the minimum wage, 0.5% more than a bank teller.

So "everyone uses these setups" means everyone is losing with them. What the few winners likely have:

layer        can we test it?
selection    PARTIALLY — proxies only, and the result below
sizing       PARTIALLY — mechanical proxies are testable: kill trades not
             in profit by bar 5-7, add after bar k. Untested.
execution    PARTIALLY — our attempt failed, but a resting order can be
             simulated directly: place the limit before price arrives, fill
             on every touch, keep the fills where price kept going
tape         NO  — lives inside the candle
regime       PARTIALLY — subperiod splits already show instability

Sizing connects to our own E9 result, which we misread. Losers peak at bars 1–7, winners around bar 40. We read that as "the exit can't be fixed." It is more likely the shape discretionary management exploits — quick kills, patient holds — and a one-unit-per-trade design is structurally unable to capture it.

Execution may be the largest term in the study, and our attempt to measure it failed. A resting limit at the level earns the half-spread instead of paying it — a swing of one full spread, several times any effect we measured. We tried to bound it and produced a look-ahead instead: entering at the VWAP level is only possible because the bar closed above it, and a real limit would fill on every touch, including all the touches where price kept falling. Those fills are absent from the sample. The attempted figure was 8× larger than the cost flip could explain, which is what gave it away. The execution question remains open, not answered.

Selection, tested with matched placebos, carries no ordering — see the section above. An earlier side-balanced version of this split produced +0.0688 and then −0.0637 depending on how the two sides were averaged; both are artifacts and the table has been removed from this note. Error 08 records what went wrong.

Ten errors, and what caught each

Listed because the pattern in how they were caught is more useful than any single result. Five of the ten were spotted because a number was far larger than its mechanism could explain, and the last four are all the same species: two arms that were not measuring the same thing.

01

The universe screened on the future

In the daily study, stocks entered the sample by dollar volume measured through the month whose return we were predicting — so names got in because they had already spiked. Worth 19 percentage points a year. Nine model-side tests passed while it was live; what exposed it was looking at the holdings and seeing nano-caps in a "top-1500 by liquidity" universe.

02

73% of "retests" were the next bar

Our detector took the first bar whose low touched the level. That's usually the bar right after the breakout — price closing above and dipping next candle, not advancing, stalling and returning. Caught by rendering six real detections and looking at them.

03

Stops so tight that friction looked like failure

Thirteen strategies all returned −0.07 to −0.14R. That convergence was the tell. Gross is +0.013 to +0.018R at every stop width (pre-fix: long-only on a non-point-in-time universe, so drift-contaminated). Gap-through slippage on stops accounts for 0.002R of the difference; the cost charge accounts for the rest. The same 2bp round trip costs −0.135R on a half-bar stop and −0.034R on a two-bar stop. We'd built the same friction into every test.

04

Standard errors that ignored the market

We reported a long-short spread as "t = 3.63" while treating 243,026 trades as independent. About 166 share each session's market move. A dated re-run gives the opposite sign. We first assumed a within-day correlation of 0.05; the measured ICC is 0.009, giving a design effect near 2.5 and an original t of roughly 2.3 — and that statistic was measuring drift on a biased universe regardless.

05

The cost hurdle was doubled

We treated quoted round-trip basis points as one-way and multiplied by two. On a 0.9% risk unit 1bp is 0.0111R, not 0.0222R. Every figure in that table was wrong by 2x, and it made us claim the confidence interval excluded an edge covering 1bp when it did not.

06

Trade-weighting conflated signal tilt with edge

The number of signals per side correlates with the day's direction — corr = +0.351, p = 2e-42. So a trade-weighted average over-weights whichever side the day rewarded, while our random baseline fires exactly one long and one short by construction. We were comparing variable tilt against no tilt.

07

Side-balancing swapped one bias for its mirror

Weighting each side equally means each trade gets weight 1/n, and n is only known at 11:00. On a trend day the minority side is a handful of counter-trend signals that mostly lose, so the fix biased the result down as hard as trade-weighting biased it up. It is also not a tradable rule — you cannot size by 1/n in advance.

08

An unbalanced average flipped a selection result

Conditioning on high relative volume appeared to give +0.0688R, t=2.84. On that thinned subsample 124 of 979 sessions had only one side firing, and our averaging let those contribute a single side against random's two. Properly balanced: -0.0637R. On the full sample it never showed, because almost every session has both.

09

A look-ahead in the placebo arm

The matched placebo could be drawn up to 12 bars before its signal, where it enters ahead of the breakout and captures the move that defines it. Those placebos earn +0.67R against the signal's -0.08R. Drawn at or after the signal instead, the difference is -0.0089R.

10

Two numbers that were never the same statistic

A one-off diagnostic and the main run reported ORB as -0.0700 and +0.0198. Our first explanation — that the diagnostic weighted signals by their candidate count — was wrong; that reweighting moves the figure by 0.005R and the correlation it needs is −0.014. The actual cause was that the diagnostic traded long breaks only, net of costs, on a tenth of the sample. A diagnostic must carry its own specification with it, or it cannot be compared to anything.

Where we know we're weak

No news calendar. One strategy we tested says explicitly: never trade during news, only after — and avoid days with no news. We can't filter on that at all. If the edge lives entirely in post-news continuation, our test cannot see it.
Single stocks, not indices. 199 of the 200 names in the point-in-time universe are individual stocks. Almost everything we tested is taught on ES, NQ and SPY. We have the futures data now (511 MB, verified back-adjusted) and haven't run a single test on it.
No Asia session. Equity data starts at 04:00 ET, so the session-rotation claim was tested against a pre-market proxy carrying 8% of daily volume — not the real thing. Futures would fix this.
No tape. 5-minute OHLCV only. No order flow, no depth, no prints. If the read that makes this work happens inside the candle, we have no access to it.
"Confirmation" is our interpretation. Every discretionary judgement got mechanised into a rule. A negative result may be a verdict on our rule rather than on your read.

What would help most

Six questions

  1. Q1
    Is the level a direction signal or a risk-unit signal? Nothing we tested beats "which way has it gone for thirty minutes." If the level's job is to tell you where the stop goes rather than which way to trade, we have been measuring the wrong thing — and we would rerun against that instead.
  2. Q2
    Is our retest definition right? We require a 0.18% advance and 3 bars of separation before a tap counts. Too strict? Too loose? What would you require?
  3. Q3
    What spread should we assume? Everything hinges on this. On our ~0.9% risk unit, 1bp round trip is 1.1% of R and 8bp is 8.9%. What do you actually pay, round trip, on a liquid name at 09:45?
  4. Q4
    Is the news filter the whole thing? If the setup only works on a catalyst day, then testing it across all 1,425 sessions measures the wrong population — and we should be building a catalyst filter, not testing patterns.
  5. Q5
    Should this be on ES/NQ instead? We have the data and haven't used it. Is testing session logic on single stocks a category error?
  6. Q6
    What are we not seeing? The honest version: if you've traded this window profitably, what's in your process that isn't in the list above?
